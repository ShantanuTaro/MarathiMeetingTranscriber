Why Sarvam beats Whisper on Marathi meeting audio

This tool was built twice. The first version ran a local Whisper on a laptop GPU: 3 GB of weights, a Silero VAD trim, silence-aware chunking, a levelling pass. The second version deleted all of it and sends the audio to Sarvam’s saaras:v4 instead. The deciding factor was not speed or cost, and it was not privacy, which the local build won outright. It was that on real Marathi meeting recordings the hosted India-first model made fewer of the mistakes a human then has to find.

Real Marathi meetings are not in Marathi

A gram sabha or a taluka review runs in Marathi with English load-bearing words dropped in whole: agenda, budget review, tender, sanction, proposal. Nobody stops to translate them, and a transcript that renders them in Latin script is not wrong so much as inconsistent: the same spoken word appears two ways depending on which side of the switch the decoder thought it was on.

This is where the two models actually differed. On the same clip, Sarvam brought the English terms back transliterated into Devanagari (agenda → अजेंडा, budget review → बजेट रिव्ह्यू), which is one script for one document. The local Whisper left them in Latin script. Almost all of the turquoise highlighting in the finished .docx exists to catch exactly that, and with Sarvam running there is simply less of it to catch.

The second, nastier version of the same problem is language detection. Left on auto, a code-switched recording is a coin flip: get it wrong in the first thirty seconds and the model commits, and an hour of Marathi comes back as an hour of confident English. That is why this pipeline pins the language explicitly rather than letting anything guess.

The failure mode that ate whole minutes

Hall audio, meaning one microphone, a ceiling fan, forty people and a PA system with its own echo, is where a general-purpose model stops degrading gracefully and starts hallucinating. The specific Whisper behaviour on this material is a repetition loop: the decoder gets stuck and emits the same fragment dozens of times, filling a real minute of discussion with प्रदावादा प्रदावादा प्रदावादा…

Whisper knows this happens. It runs a compression-ratio check internally to trigger a retry at a higher temperature, and after the last temperature it keeps the loop anyway rather than dropping it. The document builder here still carries that same check (looping(), a zlib ratio over 2.6) to mark those paragraphs, because the guard had to exist even after the loop was in the transcript. On Sarvam the guard is still armed and has had far less to do.

What the on-device version actually cost

Running the model locally is usually argued for on privacy and on price, and both arguments are real. What gets left out is the rest of the bill:

  • Three gigabytes of weights that a user has to download before the tool does anything, and a GPU to make them usable.
  • Four heavy dependencies (numpy, mlx, onnxruntime, sherpa-onnx), each with its own platform quirks. Deleting the local path is what took this project down to a 40 MB virtualenv with ffmpeg as the only binary requirement.
  • An audio pipeline of your own. VAD trimming, silence-aware chunking and a levelling pass were not decoration; a raw hour fed straight to a local model is worse than the same hour prepared. That is code that has to be maintained and tuned, and it is the part nobody counts.
  • No vocabulary bias. Sarvam takes a keyterms list, up to 50 village names, scheme acronyms and the names of the people in the room, as a decoder bias rather than as a prompt. Getting the same effect out of a local Whisper means a prompt it is free to ignore.

None of that made the local transcript better. It made the tool heavier while its Marathi stayed worse than the API it was competing with, which is a bad trade in both directions at once.

What you give up by using Sarvam

This is not a free win, and pretending otherwise is how people get surprised by a transcript later:

  • No per-word confidence. saaras:v4 reports none at all, so the yellow “the model was unsure here” highlighting is off on this backend and the review checklist can only list Latin-script words. A more accurate transcript that cannot tell you where it is weak is a real loss, not a technicality.
  • Timestamps are per sentence. Each review row’s time is interpolated across its sentence: right to the minute, not to the second.
  • The audio leaves the machine. Unconditionally, and that is the one thing the local build did better. Read the provider’s retention policy before sending a recording that matters.
  • Billing starts when the job starts. A run that comes back empty, errors, or gets stopped is billed in full, because there is no cancel endpoint to call.

When an on-device model is still right

Two cases, and they are not rare. If the recording cannot leave the building, a legal matter, an HR investigation, anything under a policy that forbids third-party processing, then local is the only answer, and the accuracy gap is a cost you absorb by budgeting more human review time. Expect Latin-script English, expect loops on hall audio, and read the whole transcript rather than a checklist.

If you need to know which words to doubt, a hosted model that reports per-word confidence is the better tool: this app ships ElevenLabs Scribe for exactly that, and its per-word logprobs are what make the yellow highlighting mean something. Its Marathi is most likely weaker than Sarvam’s. That is the trade: confidence data for accuracy, picked per upload rather than once for everybody.

How this was decided, honestly

This is one project’s experience on real Marathi meeting recordings and a test clip, not a benchmark. No word error rate was computed, no held-out set was scored, and nothing here is a claim about any model’s performance in general. What was compared was the thing that actually mattered to the people using it: how much of the finished document a human still had to fix. If you are choosing a backend for your own recordings, run one real meeting through both. The app lets you pick the backend per upload precisely so that the comparison costs an hour of audio and nothing more.
